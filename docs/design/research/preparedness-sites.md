# How the best preparedness sites look, and what a UK household-resilience app can borrow

*Researcher's report, 30 October 2026. Hex values and font names were read from live stylesheets on the day.*

## Site by site

**UK Be Ready** — https://beready.campaign.gov.uk/ (relaunched 22 Oct 2026). Pure GOV.UK campaign theme: GDS Transport, #0b0c0c text, brand blue #1d70b8, focus yellow #ffdd00, success green #0f7a52, pale surface #f4f8fb. Hero is a blue block with a stock photo. Section tiles use small flat pictograms. Headline "Are you emergency ready?"; framing "Being ready for 3 days helps you manage while support reaches those in need." Strongest idea: the checklist is grouped by need (Water, Food, Hygiene, Light and warmth, Communication, Money and documents, Medical, Baby, Pets) with a hard number per item, and the copy permits building "gradually over time". Falls flat: no identity beyond GOV.UK; the checkboxes are typographic.

**US Ready.gov / FEMA** — https://www.ready.gov/kit. USWDS palette: Source Sans Pro, Merriweather headings, #005ea2, navy #162e51, Ready green #477326. Disaster photography in a six-card grid. Strongest idea: a two-tier kit list ("Basic" then "Additional"), a printable list to take to the store, a fillable household plan. Falls flat: hazard-first navigation over 25 disaster types; stock photos of disasters, not of a prepared home.

**American Red Cross** — Roboto and Open Sans, red #e11b22, blue #007caf. Strongest idea: a readiness quiz as the entry point. Falls flat: donation promos share the page.

**New Zealand Get Ready** — https://getready.govt.nz/. The most distinctive government site. Deep blue #0b5296 and #1f476f, warning yellow #fee200, red #e0182d, pale #f6fafe; Open Sans, IBM Plex, display face Bowlby One. Bold flat illustration rather than photography (a girl with a clipboard in front of a house, arrows on the ground for the evacuation route; "Trust your danger sense" in a flooded living room). Circular hazard pictograms. Strongest idea: memorable, human campaign vocabulary ("Have a Prep Talk", "Long or Strong, Get Gone") and a plan template the household writes itself. Falls flat: campaign banners crowd the homepage; Bowlby One is loud.

**Canada Get Prepared** — standard canada.ca template. Stat anchor: "1 in 3 Canadian adults have experienced a major weather-related emergency." Worth borrowing: the 72-hour number as the entire brand.

**Sweden, "Om krisen eller kriget kommer" / MCF** — Inter on navy #10335f with warm off-white #fcfbfa and stone #d8d2c8, one burnt orange #cc4e16; restrained, editorial. The 2018 booklet used flat spot illustrations of the home; the 2024 edition (five million copies) switched to photography and added air-raid and evacuation chapters. Advice: a week of self-sufficiency and a month of medicines. Strongest idea: a calm, warm neutral palette and a posted physical object every household owns; read-aloud, easy-Swedish and sign-language editions.

**Finland 72tuntia.fi** — Bootstrap blue and Lato. Eight topic cards (power outage, food, water, sheltering, psychological resilience, communication, cybersecurity, infectious diseases), each with a custom flat illustration. Strongest idea: naming psychological resilience and reliable information as supplies alongside water.

**Tokyo Bosai (Nosigner, Good Design Gold 2016)** — construction-site yellow and black stripes, universal-design fonts, a rhino mascot, three illustration registers, stickers, a map, a flipbook. 8 million copies; the yellow-black code was adopted nationally. Strongest idea: one colour owns the category and the object is designed to be found in the dark and kept for years. Falls flat for us: hazard stripes read as warning, not reassurance.

**Australian Red Cross RediPlan** — DM Sans and Obviously Bold; near-black #100f13, red #e42313, warm grey #d9d9d6. "Small steps. Big difference." Four verbs with icons: Get in the know, Get connected, Get organised, Get packing; then a quiz. Strongest idea: a progression people can place themselves on, with packing last.

**Germany BBK** — BundesSans and BundesSerif, orange #cc4700/#ff5900 with tints #ffbd99/#ffdecc against navy #003b62. Still-life photography of ordinary groceries ("a crank radio on a table next to the guide"). "Stock up for 10 days", "14 litres of liquid per person per week", "three weeks without food, but only four days without liquid"; an official stockpile calculator at ernaehrungsvorsorge.de. Strongest idea: numbers per person per week, and still life rather than disaster.

**Sphere / UNHCR / IFRC** — practitioner tone; standards as per-person minimums (Sphere's 15 litres a person a day; UNHCR indicator tables), which is the model for a deterministic engine's outputs.

**Also**: Norway DSB Egenberedskapsuka (https://www.dsb.no/egenberedskapsuka/): FK Grotesk Neue, purple-navy #353257/#524e7b with orange #f19e4b, one week of self-sufficiency, and a campaign mark described as "the light from a lantern against a green and dark blue circle", the closest existing precedent for a lantern. Netherlands Denk Vooruit: Rijksoverheid blue #01689b and yellow #ffb612, three steps "assemble your kit, make a plan, talk about it". Artefact's Navis won Fast Company Innovation by Design 2022 for a conversational planner plus a durable home hub. No Awwwards or D&AD winner exists in this category.

## Synthesis

**Calm and distinctive versus generic.** Four traits: one owned colour, a warm neutral ground rather than white, illustration or still life of the home rather than photography of disaster, and a single number repeated everywhere (3 days, 72 hours, 10 days, a week). The generic ones inherit a government or charity design system, lead with hazard photography, and bury the household under donation or news modules.

**How readiness is visualised.** Almost nobody shows progress: typographic boxes, basic/additional tiers, four verbs with a quiz, per-person quantities, chapter tabs. No site had a meter, a timeline or a filling kit bag. That gap is the opportunity.

**Five ideas for a UK household app.**
- A lantern as the Days Ready gauge: a warm fill rising inside a lantern outline; days as the unit.
- A larder shelf, not a checklist: the need-groups as labelled jars and tins on a shelf, each with its per-person-per-day number.
- One number, everywhere: "3 days" as a typographic mark the way Finland owns "72".
- An outward-postcode weather map with the Met Office warning as the only colour.
- Four verbs as a progression, so the plan ends with the kit rather than starting with it.
